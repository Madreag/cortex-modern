Flagship

The Campaign

Cortex Command has one campaign — Conquest, the Metagame — and it is officially abandoned. This page is the committed plan for cortex-modern's third flagship: what Conquest becomes — The Frontier. How Conquest works from a direct read of the source; why it fails; what Void Wanderers proves; the game's unfinished story — a dev-canonical narrative left half-built — and why it is the campaign's real differentiator; the six combined campaign visions that were weighed, ranked with pros-and-cons tables; the architecture of the chosen design; how it rides the AI and Multiplayer flagships; the milestones; and how it is validated. The decision is recorded as ADR-012.

The headline

Open Conquest in the shipping game and a notice appears — hard-coded in Source/Menus/MainMenuGUI.cpp, ShowMetaGameNoticeScreen(): “Conquest mode is very INCOMPLETE and flawed, and the Cortex Command Community Project team will be remaking it from the ground-up in future… Alternatively, check out Void Wanderers.” The campaign is, by the team's own admission in the shipping game, unfinished — and it redirects players to a community mod. The promised rework has zero code behind it.

The campaign is the #2 community complaint, after AI. With the AI overhaul (ADR-014) and Multiplayer (ADR-015), it is now cortex-modern's third committed flagship, recorded as ADR-012 — Accepted. The chosen design is The Frontier; this page is the committed plan.

1  ·  How the campaign works today

The campaign is Conquest. Cortex Command ships one campaign — the Metagame, branded in-game as Conquest — and everything on this page is about that one thing and what it should become. Everything below is from a direct read of the campaign source on our branch: Source/Managers/MetaMan.cpp, Source/Menus/MetagameGUI.cpp, Source/Entities/MetaPlayer.* / MetaSave.*, and the Lua battle script Data/Base.rte/Activities/MetaFight.lua.

1.1   What Conquest is

Conquest is a turn-based, Risk-style map-painting game wrapped around real-time Cortex Command battles. 1–4 players (human or AI) on up to four teams contest a planet of sites — each site is a Scene flagged MetagamePlayable = 1. You own sites, collect their income, and spend gold to attack, explore or defend. Last team with a brain pool wins. That is the whole of it.

There are a handful of hand-scripted missions — the Doainar exploration run, Signal Hunt, Maginot Defence, Decision Day, Dummy Assault in Data/Missions.rte/, plus the Browncoats' bundled Refinery Assault — but they are standalone Scenario-menu entries, not chained into anything (see §4). The tutorial (GATutorial) is a hard-coded C++ activity (upstream issue #188 asks for it to become a Lua GAScripted mode). Nothing sequences any of it; the field Activity::m_InCampaignStage exists, is saved, is bound to Lua — and is read by nothing to drive a campaign. It is a hook that was never wired.

1.2   One round of Conquest

The metagame is a state machine in MetaMan::Update — GAMEINTRO → NEWROUND → REVEALSCENES → COUNTINCOME → PLAYERnTURN → BUILDBASES → RUNACTIVITIES → ENDROUND, looping until GAMEOVER. Each phase hands off to the GUI. The side tag shows where the logic lives — and that is the story of the next section:

MetaMan Reveal sites
A few more sites on the planet are uncovered each round (m_RevealRate ≈ 0.3/round).
MetagameGUI Count income
Each owned site pays Scene::GetRoundIncome(); a flat station rent is deducted. If a player would go below zero, a brain is auto-liquidated for gold.
MetagameGUI Player turn
A human allocates a defensive gold budget per owned site with a slider, and may pick one site to attack or explore. The AI turn is a single pass of MetaMan::AIPlayerTurn.
MetagameGUI Build bases
Defensive budgets are animated being spent; Scene::ApplyBuildBudget stamps bunker pieces into each site's blueprint.
MetagameGUI Run the battles
Each attacked site becomes a GAScripted "MetaFight". If a human is present it loads as a normal real-time match; otherwise AutoResolveOffensive decides it by a weighted dice roll.
MetaMan End round
IsGameOver() checks brain pools; otherwise the loop repeats. The whole metagame — including the GUI object — is auto-saved after every battle.

1.3   The battle — MetaFight.lua

Every Conquest battle you actually play is one GAScripted activity: Data/Base.rte/Activities/MetaFight.lua, ~1,900 lines. It sets up invaders and defenders, runs the standard unit AI for non-human teams, handles reinforcement deliveries and brain evacuation. Its objective is always the same: destroy the enemy brain(s). Terrain damage to the site persists to disk between rounds — the one genuinely persistent thing in the whole mode.

1.4   What works

To be fair to it: Conquest runs, and the notice's word "playable" is honest. The round FSM is sound; sites and their terrain damage round-trip to disk; MetaFight.lua is a genuinely capable, save-aware battle script; hotseat for up to four works. The skeleton of a strategic layer is real — which matters, because it means Conquest is something to build on or evolve, not only something to throw away. What is missing is everything that would make it a campaign worth being a flagship.

The code's own confession
  • The in-game notice itself — "very INCOMPLETE and flawed… remaking it from the ground-up" — hard-coded in MainMenuGUI.cpp. Upstream said it; the git history since 2024 is maintenance-only; there is no rework branch.
  • Activity::m_InCampaignStage — a field set in INIs, serialized, and bound to Lua, that nothing reads to sequence a campaign. A campaign hook that was never wired up.
  • The original "own every site" win condition is commented out in MetaMan.cpp — so a game can drag on with no decisive end.
  • MetaMan::Update ends in RTEAbort("Metagame State is out of bounds!?").
  • The notice still offers Conquest "with the A.I. and/or up to three friends" — a stale line; multiplayer was removed (ADR-011).

Conquest is not unsalvageable — the FSM and MetaFight.lua are real, working code. But it cannot be left as it is. The decision is how far to take it — that is §5.

3  ·  Void Wanderers — the campaign that actually works

The shipping game's own notice points players away from Conquest and toward Void Wanderers. It is worth understanding exactly why — it is the existing, community-validated proof that a different campaign shape works on this engine. Analysis below is from a direct read of the mod source in D:\Projects\cccp_mods\VoidWanderers.rte.

3.1   What it is

Void Wanderers (by weegee, 2013; folded into the Community Project by 4zK, 2020; still actively maintained) is a campaign total-conversion — ~865 KB of Lua across 49 scripts, explicitly inspired by FTL, X-COM, Star Command and Firefly. It is the most-played piece of Cortex Command content that is not the base game (tens of thousands of subscribers); a top community comment simply calls it "a better game than Cortex Command." It ignores the engine's Metagame entirely and builds its own persistent campaign on a single GAScripted activity, swapping scenes and scripts internally.

The premise & loop

You are a roving mercenary crew aboard a spaceship — the "Wanderers" — drifting a hostile galaxy (the "Void"). Three modes: Vessel (the ship hub — walk your commander to console "actors" to navigate, manage crew, shop), Mission (deploy an away-team to a planet for a real battle), and Assault (your ship is boarded). Fly the star map → hit a random encounter — pirates, a derelict, raiders → run a contract → return, bank loot, upgrade → fly again.

What makes it stick

Persistent crew. Soldiers are bodies in cryo-storage that carry across the whole campaign with inventory, five rank tiers and a Prestige loop (reset for a compounding bonus, then name your veteran). A leveling RPG brain with a skill tree. A reputation matrix — every contract for one faction angers another; fall far enough and they hunt your ship. A counterattack system, a black market, blueprints, ship upgrades. And it is the de-facto content aggregator of the whole CC mod scene: any installed faction mod becomes campaign content automatically.

3.2   Why it beats Conquest — and what it still lacks

Void Wanderers converts Cortex Command from a tactical sandbox into a persistent roguelite mercenary-campaign RPG. It beats Conquest on every axis the community cares about: a fiction that gives every action a reason; unit-level persistence (the killer feature — you grow attached to a named veteran); a home base you can lose; real stakes (reputation makes every job a choice); genuine mission variety; and replayability.

It sets the bar — and shows the risk

Void Wanderers is also a twelve-year-old, heavily-scripted mod, and it shows: a fragile flat-file key=value save with everything stringified; a CF-global metatable hack for cross-script state; a custom bitmap font that spawns one object per glyph; clunky walk-to-a-console UX; constant breakage against engine changes. And by its maintainers' own admission it has no real story and no endgame — the changelog calls its difficulty curve a "temporary feature until I figure out better endgame mechanics." Void Wanderers proves a shape is possible. It is a reference, not a thing to ship. Mine it, keep it green in the AI flagship's CI mod corpus — do not fork it.

4  ·  The unfinished story

Conquest has no fiction — but Cortex Command does. Dan Tabar ("Data") and Arne Niklas Jansson ("Android Arts") built a deep, deliberately half-finished sci-fi universe across 2004–2010. The planned story mode was cut: after eleven-plus years of development the team shipped only skirmish and Conquest, and the fluff doc's story scenarios were left marked "Awaiting cleanup!" Niklas, in his own words, the lore "got stuck halfway and never sprouted legs because I'm a lazy git." That unfinished story is the campaign's single biggest asset — and most of it is sitting in the game's own files.

4.1   The setting

A few centuries hence, a cybernetic humanity has met aliens and fallen into a "gold rush of galactic proportions." You are the boss of a small gold-mining outfit, newly landed on the frontier planet the game's own scene data names FE-1 — brought down by a TradeStar, the orbital marketplace-and-FTL hub the supercorporate Free Trade monopoly builds and the in-game economy revolves around ("Tradestar Midas"). War is fought brain-first: a commander is a brain in a jar, controlling cloned and robotic bodies through a universal neurotranslator — destroy the brain and the faction falls. FE-1 itself is haunted: a near-extinction event left only a cocooning native crab; the hexagonal catacombs beneath belong to the Priorus, precursor "giant wasps" who colonised the region ~8,000 years ago and vanished; and the system's star is, on a geological clock, doomed to swell. First contact — with the nature-worshipping alien S'vaari — is canon, fully fleshed out in the metagame scene text. The campaign does not invent a universe. It finishes one.

Behind it: Free Trade (the supercorporate economy), Alchiral (the organic-body and cloning monopoly), and the wider OSA neighbours the fluff sketches — the Expatriate, the Silicone Alliance, the unknowable Myskoplex.

4.3   The story is half-built — in two halves

Cortex Command already ships story fragments: five standalone missions — the Doainar exploration (with an ancient bunker hiding cartesian coordinates to somewhere); Signal Hunt, an Alchiral contract to track an unregistered cloning signal to a tampered Control Chip; the Maginot Defence → Decision Day two-parter (lose a bunker, vow revenge, assault the enemy fortress — the missions explicitly cross-reference each other); and Dummy Assault — plus the Browncoats' cinematic Refinery Assault. They prove the engine carries authored, voiced, save-aware missions. Nothing chains them.

And the dev's scrapped arc, from the fluff doc, was a clean six-beat story with "intros, midtros and extros" — never written. The campaign's narrative spine is the obvious move: chain the fragments that shipped, and finish the arc that didn't.

4.4   The anchor-mission spine

Six chapters, synthesised from Cortex Command's shipped missions and the dev's scrapped fluff — the campaign's authored backbone, with procedural contracts (§6) filling the space between:

New 1 · Prospect
Land on FE-1; the gold-rush brawl; stand up the outfit. The dev's own framing — a small mining franchisee, not a warlord — mechanised as the opening.
Shipped 2 · The Signal
An Alchiral contract: hunt an unregistered cloning signal, recover a tampered Control Chip. Built on the shipped Signal Hunt mission — the chip is the arc's MacGuffin.
To finish 3 · The Bombies
Follow the chip to a hijacked Alchiral VAT facility; corrupted half-clones — "Bombies" — pour from the tubes (Alchiral's honeypot). Finishes the dev's most-designed scrapped scenario.
To finish 4 · The Sentient Machine
An Orange Dummy training-CPU has woken, escaped a TradeStar behind a decoy, and is loose. Finishes the scrapped Orange-Dummy arc — and it is the Dummy faction's own shipped origin.
Shipped 5 · The Dying System
Free Trade pulls a neighbouring system's TradeStars; desperate Ronin flood FE-1; a bunker falls and is avenged. Built on the shipped Maginot → Decision Day two-parter.
To finish 6 · The Common Enemy
Wake Uzira from her lunar cryo-temple; the Mu-ilaak rise; the factions are forced into one alliance. The reveal: Free Trade wanted the Mu-ilaak gone because they self-replicate off-grid — "bad for business" — and Uzira has quietly held Alchiral stock for centuries.

The dev's scrapped beats are canon at the design-bible level; the chapter structure, names and gating are this campaign's proposal — the canon supplies the story, the campaign supplies the spine.

4.5   Scale, blanks, and how lore is delivered

The scale is small — deliberately. The fluff doc is explicit that the player is "acting like something along the lines of the owner of a local restaurant chain" — a franchise operator, not a galactic warlord; the planet is "a very minor brawl over scanty resources." That is a binding design constraint: the campaign is building a small outfit into a regional power. It is why a persistent mercenary company fits and why galactic 4X does not (§5).

Fill the blanks. The dev left explicit "ToDo" questions — OSA culture, which species are common, what backwater worlds look like. The campaign canonises them, extending Cortex Command's universe rather than inventing a new one.

Lore is woven, never narrated. No cutscene walls. Cortex Command's own voice — terse, corporate, the player addressed as "Contractor" or "commander," paid in fees and "year-end performance bonuses" — is delivered through briefings, debriefs, a codex that unlocks as you play, and encounter dialogue. The codex is a campaign primary surface, not a menu afterthought.

This is the differentiator. Every indie strategy game has procedural conquest. None of them has Cortex Command's brain-in-a-jar universe with a dev-canonical narrative arc literally waiting in a fluff doc marked "awaiting cleanup," and its villain faction shipped as a folder with the description "TBA." The campaign is not just a system — it is the act of finishing the story the game was always supposed to have.

5  ·  Six campaign visions — ranked

The 10 designs explored earlier were not 10 whole campaigns — most were parts. Split them: some are spines (the backbone — pick one), some are layers (bolt onto any spine — stack as many as you want). The campaign question is: pick a spine, stack layers. Below are six combined visions, each a coherent fusion, each scored for fit as cortex-modern's flagship campaign, each with a pros-and-cons table. All six bake in three things as standing layers: the lore (§4), neutral factions (a five-step disposition track — hostile / wary / neutral / friendly / allied, not a friend-or-enemy binary), and neutral bosses (apex threats owned by no side — the Doainar "mother crab" is the shipped precedent for a telegraphed, multi-phase, non-HP-kill boss).

1The Frontier — Conquest, Evolved ●●●●●

"A strategic war map over a half-charted frontier — that generates a persistent tactical campaign for the mercenary company you build."

How it plays. Two interlocking layers. A strategic layer — Conquest's bones, fixed: a territorial map of FE-1's sites and factions you and rival commanders contest, half of it starting as unexplored fog you scout outward into. And a tactical layer — each contested site is a typed campaign mission, fought with a persistent veteran company you recruit, equip, lead and lose. The map gives the company its reasons; the company makes each fight matter. A storyteller paces it; reputation and diplomacy colour it; the six-chapter anchor arc (§4.4) is its spine; the grand-strategy depth is an opt-in deepening, not a wall everyone hits.

ProsCons
  • It is Cortex Command's premise — the gold-rush prospector outfit on a frontier world is the literal setting (§4.1).
  • It genuinely fixes Conquest — keeps the territorial fantasy, supersedes the mode by containing it. M1 ships Conquest rebuilt.
  • Serves both audiences: strategy players get the war map; squad players get the persistent company.
  • Highest ceiling; the anchor arc is a free, dev-canonical narrative spine no other indie strategy game can claim.
  • Stages cleanly — every milestone ships standalone value (§9).
  • Rides the AI and Multiplayer flagships; overwhelmingly [MOD].
  • The most scope of any vision, even staged.
  • Two layers means two UIs and two loops to balance; the seam is an integration risk.
  • If under-built it risks being two mediocre halves instead of one strong whole.
  • Full payoff is back-loaded past M3.
  • Needs a competent strategic AI — a genuinely hard problem, hard-dependent on the AI flagship.

Closest to: Conquest × X-COM × Battle Brothers · vs Conquest: evolves it — Conquest is its strategic spine · Verdict: the only vision that honours "fix Conquest," fits the lore exactly, and clears the flagship bar. Chosen (ADR-012) — detailed in §6–§10.

2Last Company ●●●●●

"Not a war for territory — a war against the world. Your company is the last outfit in a dying region, and it is closing in."

How it plays. Survival is the spine: escalating cycles of pressure; you hold and harden a foothold. The persistent company is everything — every veteran lost is irreplaceable. Exploration is scouting out to scavenge and to uncover why the region is dying; a storyteller is the antagonist's hand. The faction war is gone — the great powers abandoned this place. FE-1's doomed star and its extinction-haunted past are the canon hook.

ProsCons
  • Strongest attachment and tension — named veterans, real attrition, a world that pushes back.
  • Destructible terrain makes the siege visceral — your base is genuinely chewed apart.
  • Fits the lore's doomed-planet hook (the swelling star, the extinction event) cleanly.
  • Lore is the goal — the campaign is the mystery of the cataclysm, discovered.
  • Lower scope than The Frontier; no strategic-map layer to balance.
  • Drops the territorial layer — it does not fix Conquest, it replaces it.
  • Open-ended survival risks aimlessness without a hard arc — Void Wanderers' admitted failure.
  • Can become a grind of escalating numbers.
  • The anchor arc (§4.4) fits less naturally — it is a frontier-war story, not a siege story.

Closest to: They Are Billions × X-COM × Stationeers · vs Conquest: replaces it · Verdict: excellent and focused — and it folds into The Frontier as the "Cataclysm" storyteller personality (§6, L5).

3The Deep ●●●●●

"A vast dead thing to descend into — the Priorus catacombs beneath FE-1. The campaign is going in, and the loop is discovery."

How it plays. Exploration is the spine: a layered unknown — the hexagonal Priorus catacombs, derelict bunkers, the dark — deeper meaning richer and deadlier. A persistent company is your expedition crew; a forward camp is the base you push from. The Doainar mission's ancient bunker and its cartesian coordinates to somewhere is the shipped seed. Lore is the gameplay.

ProsCons
  • Discovery is a powerful, underused hook — "what is down there" pulls the player on its own.
  • The Priorus precursors are canon and almost entirely unexplored — pure design space.
  • Fits Cortex Command's fog-of-war (upstream is already building two-stage FoW).
  • Lore-maximal — every chamber and relic is a story-piece; the codex is the point.
  • Exploration alone is thin as a spine — it needs a reason (loot, survival, story) to matter.
  • Risks "walking-simulator" pacing without combat stakes.
  • Does not fix or honour Conquest.
  • Hard to make the hundredth discovery feel like the first; needs a deep content pipeline.

Closest to: STALKER × Subnautica × the Doainar mission · vs Conquest: replaces it · Verdict: a superb chapter, a thin spine — folds into The Frontier as a Priorus-catacomb mission arc inside L2.

4The Hunt ●●●●●

"Your company hunts the apex threats no faction will touch. The bosses are not gates — they are the campaign."

How it plays. Neutral bosses elevated to the main loop — a roster of named, legendary world-threats to track and bring down. A persistent company of specialists; exploration finds the quarry; survival is the long, terrain-wrecking fight. Factions hire you and bid for trophies. FE-1's cocoon crab and its "mother crab," the Priorus wildlife, and the Mu-ilaak are the shipped bestiary seed.

ProsCons
  • A fresh, distinctive identity — nothing else in the genre is Monster Hunter on destructible terrain.
  • The Doainar "mother crab" is a shipped precedent for a multi-phase, telegraphed boss.
  • Bestiary-as-lore — every beast has a legend; the codex is a hunter's almanac.
  • Each hunt is a self-contained, screenshot-worthy set-piece.
  • Narrow — a campaign of boss fights and little else risks monotony.
  • Does not fix or honour Conquest; no strategic layer.
  • Bosses are the most content-expensive thing to author well; a thin roster shows fast.
  • The faction war — CC's core conflict — becomes set-dressing.

Closest to: Monster Hunter × Shadow of the Colossus · vs Conquest: replaces it · Verdict: a great system, a narrow spine — folds into The Frontier as first-class neutral-boss content in L4.

5The Living Frontier ●●●●●

"The Frontier's world — but run as a living simulation. The frontier wars, explores and falls whether you act or not; you are one company writing your own story in it."

How it plays. The emergent sandbox is the spine: a RimWorld-style storyteller and the simulation drive everything; no fixed win. Every system from The Frontier is present — AI factions conquer, everyone explores the frontier, survival pressure ebbs and flows, you run your company — but the world owns the story, not an authored arc.

ProsCons
  • Infinite emergent replayability — every playthrough genuinely different.
  • The storyteller is a proven pacing engine; the world feels alive.
  • Mostly [MOD] — a GlobalScript director.
  • "No fixed goal" risks aimlessness — and it strands the dev's anchor arc, the campaign's best asset.
  • A pure sandbox struggles to feel like a campaign — a campaign implies a journey.
  • Hard to onboard; intimidating without structure.
  • The storyteller is better as a layer on a spine than as the spine itself.

Closest to: RimWorld × Dwarf Fortress · vs Conquest: replaces it · Verdict: the storyteller is essential — but as L5 of The Frontier, not as the spine. Folds in as the "Living Frontier" emergent-pacing presets.

6Warlords ●●●●●

"Grand strategy as the spine — diplomacy, economy, supply, the strategic turn. A war to inherit a collapsing power."

How it plays. A deep strategic turn — research, diplomacy, declarations of war, supply lines, alliances — is the heart; Cortex Command battles resolve the clashes you manoeuvre toward. You experience it through a company, and a storyteller injects crises.

ProsCons
  • The deepest strategic experience; immense replayability for strategy players.
  • Honours and massively extends Conquest's genre.
  • A distinctive identity — no other action game carries a real wargame layer.
  • The lore actively argues against it — the dev frames the player as a franchise owner, not an empire-inheritor (§4.5). This scale is canon-wrong.
  • The coldest, most abstract vision — the tactical battle becomes a sideshow.
  • The most build-heavy; the hardest AI problem on the page; the narrowest audience.
  • Weakest on attachment — no named veteran you grow to love.

Closest to: Hearts of Iron × the AWAW wargame ruleset · vs Conquest: deepens it · Verdict: deep but cold, niche, and canon-wrong as a spine — folds into The Frontier as the opt-in grand-strategy depth on L6, off by default.

Nothing is wasted — how the five resolve into the one

The visions are not rivals; five of them are components of the first. With The Frontier as the chosen spine, the other five become its layers and modes:

  • Last Company → the "Cataclysm" storyteller personality (L5) — the dying-region pacing, available to any campaign.
  • The Deep → a Priorus-catacomb mission arc (L2) — an exploration chapter, and a side-anchor.
  • The Hunt → neutral bosses as first-class L4 content — the Doainar "mother crab" pattern, productised.
  • The Living Frontier → the storyteller's emergent-pacing presets (L5).
  • Warlords → the opt-in grand-strategy depth on the strategic layer (L6 / milestone M5), off by default.

And "fix Conquest" is honoured. The Frontier's first milestone, M1, ships Conquest rebuilt — the strategic metagame, un-broken, as a standalone release. The decision is only how far past that to go.

6  ·  The architecture — the chosen design

The rest of this page details The Frontier — the chosen design (ADR-012) — in build-from depth; the other five visions are its measuring sticks. It is built as a Cortex Command .rte module (a new Data/Campaign.rte/): a GAScripted activity for missions and the hub, and a GlobalScript that owns the persistent campaign model.

6.1   What it is

You run a mercenary company on FE-1 — not a single hero. Your command brain is the company's anchor, the one unit whose loss ends the run; everything else is crew you recruit, equip, lead, lose and mourn. Above the company sits a strategic war map — Conquest's territorial contest, rebuilt — where you and rival commanders fight for sites and factions. Each contested site is a real campaign mission your company deploys to. A storyteller paces the war; reputation and diplomacy colour it; the six-chapter anchor arc is its narrative spine; an opt-in grand-strategy depth is there for players who want the wargame. It keeps every piece of Cortex Command's identity and gives it the long-form structure the base game never had.

6.2   The layered model

Each layer is a separately-shippable piece — which is what makes the milestone plan in §9 possible. The tag is the dominant cost: almost all of it is [MOD].

UI
Campaign UI, codex & after-action  [MOD][CODE]
Cross-cutting. The strategic map & contract board, the mission briefing, the loadout planning table, the codex (lore woven, never narrated), and the post-mission debrief — a war-room "what happened and why" on the AI flagship's decision-event channel.
Answers: Conquest's slider-walls and animation-crawls — and "you won" with no causal trace.
L6
The strategic layer — the war map  [MOD]
Conquest reborn: FE-1's territorial map of sites and factions, contested by the player and rival commanders, with real model-based resolution and fog-of-war frontier. An opt-in grand-strategy depth (economy, supply, diplomacy, the strategic turn) sits on top for wargame players.
Answers: the coin-flip, the hollow strategy AI — and keeps the conquest fantasy.
L5
Storyteller / incident director  [MOD]
A RimWorld-style pacing personality — Calm, Chaotic, Ironman, Cataclysm — that schedules world events and contract pressure against an authored intensity curve, from a plug-in event registry. Pick a storyteller and the shape of the whole campaign changes.
Answers: Conquest's flat, eventless rounds — the campaign now feels authored.
L4
Factions, reputation, neutrals & the rival  [MOD]
A five-step disposition track (hostile / wary / neutral / friendly / allied) over Cortex Command's team system; reputation-gated buy access and contracts; faction territory; first-class neutral factions and neutral bosses; and a named, persistent rival commander who remembers your tactics across missions and counters them.
Answers: the hollow strategy AI, the friend/enemy binary — the world now reacts.
L3
The tactical campaign loop & hub  [MOD][CODE]
The company's persistent base; the contract board; travel; the buy / loadout / deploy loop — the campaign's "between-missions" space and the diegetic dropship delivery into a mission.
Answers: nowhere to be a company — Conquest has only a planet to click.
L2
The mission engine & the anchor arc  [MOD]
A typed mission manifest — one contract format for hand-authored, procedural and modded missions — with typed objectives (breach, hold, escort, sabotage, rescue, extract…), a Left 4 Dead-style pacing director, destruction-aware authoring rules, and the six-chapter anchor arc (§4.4) seeded here.
Answers: the no-content-format and one-objective repetition of Conquest.
L1
The persistent roster  [MOD]
Veterans: actors that survive a mission carry forward with XP, traits, named identity and visible scars. Rescue-first permadeath — a downed soldier can be saved, but once truly gone is gone and remembered. The command brain as the run's anchor.
Answers: disposable counters — the campaign now becomes yours.
L0
Activity & persistence substrate  [MOD]
A GAScripted activity plus a GlobalScript that owns the campaign model; all campaign state serialised through the engine's compressed .ccsave path with a real typed schema — not a flat file.
Answers: the model welded into the GUI; the fragile, stringly-typed mod saves.

L0–L5 are the tactical mercenary campaign; L6 (blue) is the strategic war map that drives it — the two loops of The Frontier. The UI layer is cross-cutting (dashed). Layer order is dependency order — it is also the milestone order in §9.

6.3   The tactical loop

What the player does inside one mission — the counterpart to Conquest's round in §1.2:

Hub Take a contract
Pick a job from the contract board — surfaced by the strategic layer (L6), the storyteller (L5) and the anchor arc — each a typed mission manifest with a faction, a reward and a reputation consequence.
Hub Plan the loadout
Compose the squad from the persistent roster; recruit and equip. The loadout screen is a planning table, not just a shop.
Mission Deploy
The physical dropship delivery into the mission scene — kept diegetic; the craft can still be shot down. Cortex Command's signature entry, unchanged.
Mission Command the battle
The real-time fight: direct control and squad command on the new AI, against typed objectives and the rival commander, with the mission director pacing the pressure.
Mission Extract
Rescue who you can, salvage what you can, get the command brain out. Who lives and who is left behind is decided here.
Hub Debrief
The after-action report — what happened and why, drawn from the AI flagship's decision-event log and replay. New codex entries unlock here, woven not narrated.
Hub Advance
Veterans gain XP and scars; reputation shifts; the strategic map updates; the storyteller schedules what comes next. The board refreshes — back to step 1.

Above this loop, the strategic loop turns more slowly — the war map advances, rival commanders move, factions rise and fall — and that is what generates the contracts the tactical loop consumes. The design target: pressure inside the mission, curiosity after it.

7  ·  The engine & mod work

The campaign is, deliberately, almost entirely a [MOD] — a major argument for The Frontier. Cortex Command's engine already ships the surface a campaign needs; most of it has simply never been assembled.

[MOD]  What ships as Lua / data

  • The whole campaign model — roster, missions, factions, reputation, storyteller, the war map — on GAScripted + GlobalScript.
  • The typed mission manifest, the procedural generator, and the six anchor missions.
  • The hub, contract board, briefings, codex and debrief, drawn with PrimitiveMan.
  • Faction content — Cortex Command factions are already .rte modules, Uzira.rte and MuIlaak.rte among them.
  • Campaign persistence through the existing .ccsave path.

[CODE]  The little that needs the engine

  • The dormant v6.0 primitives — capture points, buy doors, dropship docks, item dispensers, AI tactics — exist but are unused; wiring them in is mostly data and Lua.
  • A polished campaign-map screen — a retained-widget strategic map wants engine UI support; rides roadmap T3-03.
  • Save hooks for roster and campaign state — shared with the AI flagship's persistent-order serialisation (§8).
  • A custom-property mechanism converts further campaign features from [CODE] into [MOD] — see ADR-010.
First, extract the model from the GUI

The one piece of non-campaign work that must happen regardless of the vision chosen is M0's: lift Conquest's rules — SetupOffensives, AutoResolveOffensive, income, finalisation — out of the ~6,000-line MetagameGUI into a small, testable model, and stop the save format serialising the GUI object. This is what makes Conquest patchable and what stops the old mode being a millstone under the new one. It is bounded, mechanical [CODE] — and the prerequisite for everything else.

Cortex Command's twenty-year .rte ecosystem is the fork's biggest asset, and the campaign is designed around it: factions are already .rte modules; the mission manifest is itself a modding format — the same typed contract the first-party campaign uses is what a modder authors a mission or a whole faction questline with. Void Wanderers stays in the AI flagship's CI mod corpus, so "does Void Wanderers still load and run" is a measured, every-build guarantee.

8  ·  Riding the AI and Multiplayer flagships

The campaign is the third flagship by design. It is sequenced last because it is the one that consumes the other two — and built right, it makes all three add up to more than three features.

Campaign × AI — the AI is the floor the campaign stands on

"A campaign is only as good as its battles," and bad AI is the #1 campaign-blocker — a campaign on today's AI is babysitting, not commanding. The AI overhaul (ADR-014) — a locomotion-first rebuild of the AI's Lua — is a hard dependency, and the two designs interlock milestone-for-milestone:

  • The AI flagship's M1 (locomotion) — actors that actually follow the paths they are given — is the floor: it is the milestone that turns commanding a campaign squad from babysitting into command.
  • The AI flagship's M3 (squad coordination) — real squads, not "stand near the leader and copy its buttons" — is what the campaign's tactical battles run on; the campaign's persistent rival commander (L4) is the strategic layer that directs those enemy squads and carries a grudge across missions.
  • The AI flagship's M5 (orders & command) is doubly load-bearing: its wider order vocabulary — hold, breach, retreat, rescue, mine, escort — is the campaign's mission-objective vocabulary; its persistent orders serialise into .ccsave, which is the campaign's mid-mission save; and its non-destructive direct-control hand-off lets a player take and release a unit without wiping its brain.
  • The AI flagship's M0 — the decision-event channel, replay capture and the headless harness — is the campaign's after-action debrief and codex-unlock infrastructure: "show me why I lost that squad," the same channel.
Campaign × Multiplayer — the campaign is the content co-op ships on

The Multiplayer flagship (ADR-015) is not a separate world — its milestones M3 and M4 are literally validated by "complete a co-op campaign mission."

  • A co-op campaign must save and resume mid-mission — the campaign and Multiplayer M4 share one .ccsave path; build it once.
  • Multiplayer is server-authoritative; the campaign's mission, director and storyteller state are "Truth" in that authority model — server-owned, never client-divergent.
  • The strategic war map (L6) is exactly the async / strategic-layer multiplayer the Multiplayer page flags as "a fine complement for a persistent campaign — shared meta, no live sync."
  • The campaign's new state — roster, mission, faction, storyteller — is built to the determinism-island contract from the start, so Multiplayer inherits it free.
One harness, three flagships

The AI and Multiplayer flagships already share one M0 — a decision-event channel, replay capture, a headless scenario runner, a per-tick checksum, CI. The campaign uses the same harness: its missions are headless-runnable scenarios, its debrief is the replay, its determinism is the checksum. Build the observability harness once; all three flagships gate on it. The campaign is the capstone — the AI makes its battles worth fighting, Multiplayer makes them worth fighting together, the campaign is the reason to keep playing.

9  ·  The plan — milestones

This is the committed plan. Like the AI and Multiplayer flagships it is sequenced by dependency and gated by validation, not calendar time. Every milestone is a complete, shippable improvement — and the staging keeps the lesser visions reachable too: stopping after M1 is "Conquest, Rebuilt"; the war-map deepening in M5 is the Warlords grand-strategy layer. The campaign depends on the AI flagship reaching its order-system milestone, and on Multiplayer M4 for co-op.

10  ·  Validation

A campaign cannot be "tested by playing it once" — it is too long. Like the AI and Multiplayer flagships, it is built so it is proven by machine, on the shared M0 harness, on every CI cycle:

#CheckWhat it proves
CMP-01Full campaign, headlessA whole campaign — the six-chapter arc — plays start-to-finish with no softlock, no dead-end, no unwinnable state.
CMP-02Save round-tripThe .ccsave captures and restores the entire campaign — roster, factions, storyteller, war map, mid-mission — faithfully.
CMP-03Every mission typeEach objective template completes when played by competent AI on both sides.
CMP-04Destruction rerouteOpen a hole between any two named mission regions — the mission and its AI still complete. Terrain is never the only lock.
CMP-05Storyteller differentiabilityTwo storytellers on one seed produce measurably different runs — the personality is real, not a label.
CMP-06Director pacingMission intensity stays inside its authored band — no flatlines, no unrelenting peaks.
CMP-07Strategic cascadeReputation thresholds, faction shifts and war-map turns resolve cleanly — no deadlocks, no impossible states.
CMP-08Co-op missionTwo players complete a co-op campaign mission across the network — shared with Multiplayer M4.
CMP-09DeterminismThe same seed produces the same procedural mission and event stream — replays reproduce.

The human role is the irreducible minimum the AI and Multiplayer pages also draw: authoring the anchor missions and tuning the storytellers, spot-checking the saved replay of any failure, and the final "is it fun" judgement. The machine checks against the bar; the human sets it.

11  ·  What not to do

  • Don't leave the rules in the GUI. Extract the metagame model first (M0). Every vision, even just patching Conquest, is blocked behind a 6,000-line god-object until this is done.
  • Don't discard Conquest reflexively. Its FSM and battle script are working code; the recommended plan keeps and rebuilds the strategic layer. "Replace" is one path among six, not the default.
  • Don't fork Void Wanderers. It proves the shape and stays in the CI corpus — learn from it, build fresh. Forking means owning its twelve-year-old architecture forever.
  • Don't waste the lore. Cortex Command's unfinished story is the differentiator. Don't ship a campaign that ignores the dev-canonical arc — and don't narrate it in cutscenes; weave it.
  • Don't scale up to a galactic 4X. The dev's own framing is a franchise owner, not a warlord. The campaign builds a small outfit into a regional power — that is the canon scale.
  • Don't ship a campaign with no arc. A pure sandbox or pure survival has no destination; the storyteller and survival belong as components and modes, not as the whole.
  • Don't ship coin-flip combat. Every battle that matters is played or resolved by a real model — never a funds-weighted dice roll.
  • Don't build the campaign before the AI floor. A campaign on bad AI is babysitting. The AI flagship is a hard dependency.
  • Don't invent a flat-file save. Use .ccsave with a typed schema on the determinism contract — so Multiplayer inherits it free.
  • Don't make the grand-strategy layer mandatory. It is the opt-in depth of the war map (L6, M5). Forced on everyone, it rebuilds Conquest's coldness.
  • Don't gate fun behind grind or power-creep. Progression is horizontal — each unlock opens a tactic, never erases a role or inflates a number.
The decision — recorded

The campaign is cortex-modern's third committed flagship, alongside the AI overhaul (ADR-014) and Multiplayer (ADR-015). It is recorded as ADR-012 — Accepted.

The chosen design is The Frontier: Conquest's strategic war map, fixed and evolved, with a persistent tactical company campaign hanging off it, the storyteller pacing it, and the game's own unfinished story — the dev-canonical six-chapter arc — as its spine. It is the one vision that fixes Conquest, fits Cortex Command's literal premise, and clears the flagship bar; it is staged so M1 alone ships a rebuilt Conquest and retires the in-game apology notice, and the five other visions all fold into it as layers and modes (§5).

The starting point is M0 — extract the metagame model from the MetagameGUI god-object and stand up the campaign harness. From there the milestone plan in §9 runs M0–M6, every milestone a shippable release, gated by the CMP campaign-trust suite (§10).
