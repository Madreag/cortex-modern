Foundations

The AI Overhaul

Cortex Command's AI is the game's most-cited weakness — and the one the upstream lead has openly called garbage. This page is the full picture: exactly how the AI works today from a direct read of the source, what is broken and why, and the complete menu of solutions — easy Lua wins, hybrid engine work, and the deep refactors — for building an AI that is actually fun to play against.

The headline

AI quality is the #1 complaint across Steam reviews, the forums and mod.io, and it has been for twenty years — old reviews still bite (GameSpy: "terrible AI"; NoobFeed: "units will get stuck or mess up or even die due to a flaw in the AI"). The live upstream tracker carries it now: #208 squad logic, #121 post-rework sentries that twitch and aim randomly, #100 squads that can't fire gatling guns.

And the project lead, Causeless, said it himself in the dev Discord (2025-08-09): "the AI is such garbage. it's very frustrating. I want to just fucking delete all the path following code."

That is a rare thing — a major subsystem the people who own it openly call broken, have sketched a fix for, and have not built. Almost all of the tactical fix is Lua and ships as a [MOD]. But making the AI genuinely good also means giving the engine new senses and a new spine. Both paths are mapped below.

1  ·  How the AI works today

Before fixing it, see it clearly. Everything below is from a direct read of the AI source on our branch: ~5,830 lines of Lua under Data/Base.rte/AI/, plus the C++ pathfinder, the controller abstraction and the invocation harness. The shape that emerges is unusual — and it explains both why the AI is so moddable and why it is so hard to make good.

1.1   The execution pipeline

Every AI actor is ticked through the same seven-step path each sim frame:

C++ Controller::ShouldUpdateAIThisFrame()
Throttle. An actor's AI only runs every AIUpdateInterval sim frames, and actors are spread across frames by ID so the cost is even. There is no emergency interrupt — a falling actor waits its turn like everyone else.
C++ MovableMan::UpdateControllers()
Dispatch. Runs ThreadedUpdateAI across the thread pool — one worker thread per Lua state — then a serial UpdateAI pass. Most AI runs multithreaded.
LUA HumanAI.lua → NativeHumanAI:Update()
A ~10-line entry script hands off to the real controller object. (Crabs, turrets, dropships and rockets each have their own equivalent.)
LUA behaviour coroutines
The dispatcher watches the actor's AIMode and runs up to two behaviour coroutines, drawing on the HumanBehaviors / SharedBehaviors libraries.
LUA Controller:SetState(...)
The behaviour's only output: it sets the actor's Controller — the same buttons and analog sticks a human holds.
C++ Actor locomotion / physics update
The engine reads that Controller and moves the actor — exactly as if a gamepad had sent the input.
C++ PathFinder (background thread pool)
In parallel, path requests the Lua AI raised resolve on a background pool and the result is handed back for the next tick.

1.2   The C++ / Lua split

The single most important fact about CC's AI: the AI never moves an actor. It does exactly one thing each frame — set the actor's Controller, the same buttons and sticks a human holds. The engine then reads that Controller. The AI literally plays the game through the gamepad. That is why it is so moddable, and why so much of it is Lua:

The engine (C++) ownsThe Lua scripts own
The threaded per-frame invocation harness; the Controller input abstraction; the AIMode enum and waypoint storage. Every tactical decision. Which behaviour to run, and when to switch.
Sensing primitives — CastMORay, CastStrengthRay, LookForMOs — and the alarm-event list. Target acquisition, threat scoring, the "have I lost my target?" logic.
Pathfinding: the MicroPather A*, the node grid, the async request API. Path-following, locomotion, the jetpack-jump predictor, stuck handling, digging decisions.
Terrain-change tracking that feeds the pathfinder. All combat — aiming, target leading, fire / hold-fire, reloading, throwing, weapon choice.
The actor's own physics, locomotion gaits and damage model. Squad behaviour, alarm processing, tool / medikit use, gold-digging, brain-hunting.

The implication. The expensive, low-level parts — pathfinding, sensing, threading — are already in C++. The brain — all judgement — is Lua. So the tactical fix is moddable. But the AI is only as good as the senses and tools the engine hands it, and today those are thin (see §2). A genuinely good AI needs work on both sides of this line.

1.3   AI modes — the entire order vocabulary

The C++ AIMode enum (Actor.h) is the AI's whole vocabulary. A player's pie-menu order, an activity's spawn script — all any of them can say to an actor is one of these:

ModeApplies toWhat it does
SENTRYactorsHold position and facing; sweep aim; react to threats and alarms.
PATROLactorsWalk back and forth between obstacle-bounded extents.
GOTOactorsMove to a waypoint, then revert to SENTRY on arrival.
BRAINHUNTactorsSeek and destroy the nearest enemy brain.
GOLDDIGhumansScan for gold and dig toward it.
SQUADactorsFollow a leader (see §1.8).
RETURN / STAYcraftReturn to orbit / stay landed and hover.
DELIVER / BOMBcraftDescend & unload cargo / make a bombing run.
SCUTTLEcraftSelf-destruct immediately.

Plus two sentinels (NONE, COUNT). Six modes apply to fighting actors. That is the entire set of intentions a player or designer can express — there is no "hold this line", "breach here", "retreat", "rescue him", "mine that vein". Widening this vocabulary is itself a significant part of the overhaul.

1.4   The behaviour engine — coroutine soup

Within a mode, behaviour is run by Lua coroutines — not a behaviour tree, not a formal state machine. NativeHumanAI runs two coroutines at once: a main Behavior slot (combat, sentry, gold-dig) and a GoToBehavior slot (movement), so an actor can walk toward a target and shoot at the same time. A behaviour does one frame of work, calls coroutine.yield(), and after every yield checks an abort flag. When the AI wants to drop a behaviour it resumes it once more with that flag set — a cooperative protocol that exists, the source says repeatedly, "to avoid memory leaks".

BehaviourLibraryRole
GoToWptSharedBehaviorsThe ~650-line path-follower — the single most complex function in the AI. Walking, jumping, crawling, digging, stuck handling.
ShootTargetHumanBehaviorsThe main ranged-combat behaviour — aim, lead, fire decisions, weapon juggling.
SentryHumanBehaviorsGuard: face the likely approach, sweep aim, periodically sharp-aim, return to post.
AttackTarget / ThrowTargetHumanBehaviorsMelee / digger attack; grenade throwing with a ballistic solver.
WeaponSearch / ToolSearchHumanBehaviorsFind a dropped weapon or digger, score it via async pathing, go pick it up.
GoldDig / BrainSearchHuman / SharedScan a fan of rays for gold; enumerate enemy brains and route to the easiest-reached one.
ProcessAlarmEvent / FaceAlarmSharedBehaviorsHearing — turn toward, or suppress, a heard noise.

1.5   Perception — two sets of eyes

CC's AI has two completely separate spotters, and which one an actor gets is decided once, at spawn:

  • LookForTargets — the default. Casts one ray, in a random direction, per AI tick. An enemy can stand in the open and not be seen for many frames in a row. This is not a bug — it is the design for ordinary actors.
  • CheckEnemyLOS — the good one. Enumerates every enemy in a screen-sized box and checks line-of-sight to one each frame, so it reliably sees anything on screen. Only brains, snipers, bosses and high-skill (Nuts difficulty) actors are given it.

So AI competence is bimodal: most enemies you fight are running the random-ray spotter. Hearing is separate — weapons fire and explosions push AlarmEvents that ProcessAlarmEvent range-checks and occludes against terrain. When more than one target is in view, CalculateThreatLevel scores them by distance, whether they are armed, and damage taken — and a new target only displaces the current one if it out-scores it by a margin.

1.6   Locomotion & path-following

All ground movement runs through GoToWpt. It reads the engine-built path (MovePath), walks toward the next point, and pops points as it arrives. For jetpack actors it runs a genuine trajectory predictor: it estimates thrust, simulates four candidate jump arcs plus a "do nothing and fall" arc, casts obstacle rays along each, and jumps only if a jump beats falling by a margin. If the actor carries a digger and terrain blocks the path, it sweeps the digger across the obstacle.

Its weak point is stuck recovery. A StuckTimer notices the actor has stopped moving — and the "recovery" is to flail: random lateral-direction swaps, a random chance per tick to drop prone, a random chance to refuel. There is no diagnosis, no re-path, no "step back and try another way". The code around it is openly distrusted by its own authors (see §2).

1.7   Pathfinding — the deepest tech debt

Pathfinding is A* over a uniform grid of PathNodes, via the third-party MicroPather library. Edge cost is the strongest material between two nodes; terrain too hard for the actor to dig is multiplied by 1000 — effectively a wall. The pather is "jump-aware" … in theory. In practice:

  • Jump height is faked. Actor::EstimateJumpHeight() returns FLT_MAX — the comment calls it a "Sentinel value that is explicitly checked for within pathfinder code". The pather therefore assumes every actor can jump infinitely high and plans routes the actor's real locomotion then cannot follow.
  • It is not incremental. MicroPather has no incremental update — any terrain change forces a fresh A*. Scene::UpdatePathFinding reconciles changed terrain every 100 ms, capped at 100 nodes per call, and the code itself warns this "can indefinitely block updates if pathing requests are made every frame".
  • It is rebuilt constantly. A pather is created per worker thread and reset on essentially every request (dig strength varies per actor). The source carries the TODOs "… (or write our own pather)!" and "… we create a new pather for every thread!".

In a game whose entire identity is terrain that changes every second, a pathfinder that cannot cheaply react to terrain changing is the root cause of more visible failures than any other single thing.

1.8   Squad AI — there isn't any

There is exactly one squad mechanic, and it is not coordination. A squadmate standing near its leader literally copies the leader's controller states — move, jump, prone — and fires when the leader fires. That is the whole of it. No roles. No formation. No spreading out. No deciding who shoots whom. No shared knowledge of the battlefield. "Squad" means "stand near the leader and press the same buttons." When the leader dies, squadmates fall back to a cached waypoint or just stop. This is why squads clump, friendly-fire, and stall on each other — and it is upstream issue #208.

The code's own confession

The clearest evidence the AI needs an overhaul is in the source comments. Verbatim, from our branch:

  • // TODO: Enhance MicroPather to add that capability (or write our own pather)! — PathFinder.cpp.

  • The comment -- Tell the coroutines to abort to avoid memory leaks appears more than a dozen times across the AI scripts.

This is not a subsystem that needs tuning. It needs an architecture.

3  ·  The solution space

There is a mature, well-documented body of game-AI engineering for exactly these problems. The question is not "is this solvable" — it is which techniques fit a 2D, single-threaded-sim, fully-destructible-terrain game with a Lua modding culture. A survey of ~80 sources — GDC talks, the Game AI Pro series, shipped-game post-mortems — scores them like this:

3.1   The techniques, scored for Cortex Command

TechniqueFitRole it should play here
Utility AI / IAUS●●●●●The tactical decision-maker. Score every option by weighted "considerations"; pick the best. Replaces the AIMode-enum dispatch — and every score is a loggable reason.
Influence maps●●●●●The shared spatial world-model. Threat / alarm / friendly-density / danger grids. Biases pathfinding and feeds utility scores. Fixes clumping, over-suppression, self-preservation.
D* Lite + HPA*●●●●●The incremental pathfinder. Repairs only the changed region instead of a full re-A*. HPA* regions for long hauls. Built for exactly this problem.
Behaviour trees●●●●●The behaviour-execution layer. A tick model that maps cleanly onto today's coroutines — wrap each Lua behaviour as a tree leaf. Visualizable. Not a decision-maker on its own.
HTN planning●●●●●The squad / commander layer only. Decompose "hold the brain" into squad goals. Too brittle for per-actor use under per-frame chaos.
GOAP planning●●●●●A sub-planner for bounded sequences — breach-and-enter, dig-to-goal. Destructible terrain invalidates plans too fast for it to be top-level.
Machine learning (RL)●●●●●Offline only — QA, regression testing, tuning utility weights. Never at runtime: opacity, non-determinism, retraining cost when a mod adds content.
LLMs●●●●●Flavour dialogue and an optional async "coach" only. Never in the tactical loop — latency, cost, determinism, offline play.

No single technique wins. The answer the survey, the shipped-game evidence (Halo, Killzone 2, Horizon Zero Dawn) and our own code all point to is a layered hybrid — each technique doing the one job it is best at. That architecture is §4.

3.2   Three ways to build it

The owner has put every option on the table, including refactoring the engine. So, honestly, the three implementation strategies:

[MOD]  Pure Lua

Fix the behaviour layer in place — better behaviours, real stuck recovery, reason labels — touching no C++.

Reaches: all the easy wins in §5. Cannot reach: the incremental pathfinder, influence maps, a true per-tick reflex, a real blackboard. Its ceiling is a tidier version of today's coroutine soup. Verdict: necessary, not sufficient.

[MOD][CODE]  Hybrid — recommended

C++ gains the new machinery — incremental pathfinder, influence-map grids, a blackboard, a decision-event channel, a utility/BT kernel. Lua stays the brain: which considerations apply, what behaviours exist, the leaf actions.

This is what every shipped tactical game does, and it keeps Lua — the fork's modding moat — intact. Verdict: the answer.

[ARCH]  Pure C++

Move the whole AI into the engine for maximum runtime performance and control.

The cost: it kills the Lua modding ecosystem — CC's single biggest competitive advantage. Door Kickers 2's un-moddable AI is a documented player complaint. Verdict: rejected — do not throw away the moat.

So: hybrid. The principle — the engine owns the machinery, Lua owns the judgement. C++ gives the AI fast senses, memory and a pathfinder; Lua decides what to do with them and stays open to modders. Everything from §4 on assumes this split.

4  ·  The recommended architecture

A layered stack — each layer a separate, shippable piece, each doing one job. Read it bottom-up: the fast, dumb, safety-critical layers at the bottom; the slow, smart, strategic ones at the top; an always-on debug layer across everything.

L5
Squad & Commander  [ARCH]
Per faction, ~2 Hz. An HTN-style commander reads the influence maps, decomposes objectives into squad goals, hands out positions and breach-synchronisation cues.
Fixes: clumping, friendly fire, no flanking, no real squads.
L4
Tactical Decision  [MOD][CODE]
Per actor, event-driven. Utility AI: each option scored by Lua-registered considerations through response curves; highest score wins, with an inertia bonus against flip-flop.
Fixes: dumb tool/weapon choice — and gives every decision a logged reason.
L3
Behaviour Execution  [CODE]
Per actor. A behaviour-tree kernel executes the chosen behaviour. Existing Lua behaviours become tree leaf-actions — mods keep working.
Fixes: the leak-prone, un-debuggable coroutine soup.
L2
Navigation  [CODE]
Incremental D* Lite pathfinding + HPA* regions for long hauls + a dig planner for blocked goals + a jetpack-arc planner. Influence-aware cost: "safe path" vs "fast path".
Fixes: stale paths, absurd routes, the faked jump height.
L1
Perception & World Model  [CODE]
Shared per faction. Influence maps (threat, alarm, friendly-density, danger); a per-actor blackboard (target, last-seen, ammo, order); alarm decay over time and distance.
Fixes: blind bots, over-suppression, no memory of the battlefield.
L0′
Reflex  [CODE]
Per actor, every tick, sub-millisecond. Interrupts everything above to dive from a live grenade, step out of lava or off a falling-rock arc, out of a friendly's line of fire.
Fixes: no self-preservation — units standing in obvious death.
L0
Replay & Debug — cross-cutting  [CODE]
Always on. A per-agent decision event log; an in-game overlay showing intent, target, path and the chosen behaviour's reason; influence-map overlays; hot-reload of AI tables.
Fixes: "the AI did something dumb and nobody knows why." Build this first.

Each layer is independently useful: the reflex layer alone stops the most embarrassing deaths; influence maps alone fix clumping; D* Lite alone fixes the routing. They do not have to land together — which is what makes the build order in §7 possible.

5  ·  The work — easy, medium & hard wins

Everything here is real and on the table; the owner has confirmed the engine itself can be refactored to get this right. The wins are grouped by cost, not importance — the build order in §7 interleaves them. Tags: [MOD] Lua only · [CODE] bounded C++ · [ARCH] a large rework.

6  ·  What the engine needs from us

A better AI is not only better Lua. Today the AI is starved — it has one random ray for eyes, no memory beyond coroutine locals, no map of where danger is, and a pathfinder that cannot keep up with the terrain. The largest gains need the engine to give the AI more to think with. Concretely, the new C++:

New engine capabilityWhat it gives the AI
An incremental pathfinder (D* Lite) — and a real EstimateJumpHeight.Routes that survive destructible terrain, and routes the actor can actually walk.
An influence-map grid subsystem with a Lua API.Spatial awareness — where the threats, allies, alarms and danger are.
A typed per-actor blackboard.Memory — a stable place to remember the target, the plan, what hurt it.
A perception / spatial-query API — batched LOS, cover scoring, nearest-safe-cell.Real senses, instead of one random ray cast per frame.
An emergency high-priority AI tick.The reflex layer — the ability to react now instead of waiting for the throttle.
An AI decision-event channel + the debug overlay.Explainability — the AI, and we, can see why it chose what it chose.
A utility-scoring kernel and a behaviour-tree kernel, both driven by Lua-registered considerations and leaf actions.Judgement and structured execution — kept fully moddable.

Each item is a bounded [CODE] addition; together they are the [ARCH] AI subsystem of H5. None of it removes Lua. Lua stays the surface modders — and we — author behaviour in. The engine's job is to give that Lua a brain to run on: senses, memory, a map of danger, and a pathfinder that keeps up.

7  ·  Build order

The layers do not land together — and the order matters. Each step is independently shippable and is chosen so the next step can be measured:

  1. The debug overlay & decision log (M1, E1). First, always. You cannot fix what you cannot see, and "the AI did something dumb for no visible reason" is the root of every complaint.
  2. The easy Lua wins (E2–E8). Stop the AI embarrassing itself with what is already moddable.
  3. Influence maps (M2). Cheap, fast, high-impact — fixes clumping and over-suppression at once.
  4. Incremental pathfinding (H1). The largest correctness-and-performance win; everything navigates better the moment it lands.
  5. The utility decision layer (M3). The largest "the AI feels smart" win.
  6. The reflex layer (M4). Polish that removes the most player-visible deaths.
  7. The behaviour-tree engine (H3). Retire the coroutine soup incrementally — Lua leaves keep mods working throughout.
  8. The squad / commander layer (H4), then HPA* + the dig planner (H2). Coordination last — only once individual actors are competent.

Measure every step. The AI research vault defines a 12-scenario trust suite — AI-01 defend the brain, AI-02 breach a door, AI-04 recover from a collapsed tunnel, AI-08 hold formation, AI-11 retreat from an unwinnable breach, and so on — every scenario run on destructible terrain with a replay and a reason log. Each build step is graded against it. That is how an overhaul this large stays honest.

8  ·  What not to do

  • Don't rip out Lua. The mod ecosystem is the fork's biggest asset; Door Kickers 2's un-moddable AI is a documented player complaint. Add structure to Lua — don't delete it.
  • No LLMs or runtime ML in the tactical loop. Latency, cost, non-determinism (it would break replays and any future multiplayer), offline play, and modding breakage. ML and LLMs are fine offline — tuning, QA — or for flavour dialogue. Never for deciding a shot.
  • GOAP is not the top level. A destructible world invalidates plans every blast. Use GOAP only as a sub-planner for bounded sequences (breach-and-enter, dig-to-goal).
  • HTN is not per-actor. Too brittle under per-frame chaos. Use it only at the squad/commander layer.
  • Not pure behaviour-tree, not pure utility. Utility decides what; the tree executes how. Either one alone re-discovers the other's problem the hard way.
  • No JPS+, no Recast/Detour. JPS+ assumes static, uniform-cost grids; Recast is a 3D navmesh system. Neither fits 2D pixel-destructible terrain.
  • No mega-PR. Each win in §5 ships and is measured on its own. Upstream's branch graveyard is what big-bang rewrites become here.
  • Don't skip explainability. If only one thing ships, it is the overlay. An AI you cannot see cannot be fixed.

9  ·  The phased plan

Phase 0 — See it, then steady it. The decision log and debug overlay, plus the easy Lua wins (E1–E8). Stops the AI embarrassing itself with what is already moddable — and, crucially, lets you watch it. Mostly [MOD]; ships in the near term.

Phase 1 — Senses and a spine. Influence maps (M2) and incremental pathfinding (H1) — the perception/world model and a pathfinder that survives destructible terrain. This is where bots stop walking into walls.

Phase 2 — The decision brain. The utility layer (M3), the reflex layer (M4), the blackboard (M5), perception queries (M6), cover (M7) and the behaviour-tree engine (H3). This is where the AI starts to feel smart — and every choice it makes becomes explainable.

Phase 3 — Coordination. The squad/commander layer (H4), hierarchical paths and the dig planner (H2), folded into a first-class AI subsystem (H5). Real squads — and the foundation the campaign and co-op multiplayer both stand on.

Roadmap item T1-06 is the Phase 0 squad slice. The whole effort is flagship-scale, and it is cortex-modern's recommended first real build: highest leverage, lowest risk to start, almost pure [MOD] at the front, and a problem upstream has openly admitted and not fixed. AI sits under the other two flagships — the Campaign is only as good as its battles, and co-op Multiplayer runs enemy AI server-side. Fix the AI and both get better for free.
